# Working with speakers

The most valuable part of any translator project isn't the computer. It's the
people who speak the language — who write sentences, check drafts, and say
*"no, we'd say it like this."* If that's you, this page is about you, and for
the people working beside you.

## Ask each person

Every sentence came from someone. Before a translator learns from anyone's
words, ask them — or, if that isn't possible, ask the people who decide for
them.

- **Ask in plain words** what the sentences will be used for, who will see
  them, and that they can say no.
- **Write it down.** The rails keep a place for agreements: who agreed to
  what, and when. The note beside each file records whether the speakers
  agreed — *yes*, *not yet*, or *some said no* — and the tools obey it.
- **"Not yet" is a fine answer.** The rails set those sentences aside and
  carry on, and ask again later.
- **When someone has passed on**, their words are a question for their family
  and community, never something to assume.
- **People can change their minds.** Make it easy for them to.

## Respect their time

Checking translations is real work. The rails ask how much time a speaker can
give before planning anything, size each round to it, and never push for
more. If the time looks like too little to reach your goal, they say so
honestly, so you can decide together — or find more hands through
[Join a project](/docs/join-a-project).

Short sessions work better than long ones. Checking gets harder when you're
tired, and a tired *"yes"* teaches the translator the wrong thing. Marking a
draft **?** — *not sure* — is always allowed.

## Credit, and payment

- **Credit people the way they want** — by name, by family, by community, or
  not at all. Ask.
- **Paying speakers for their time is right**, and in your project it's for
  the people leading it to decide. Champollion takes no share of anything a
  community earns, and anything a sponsor gives passes straight through.

## Sacred and restricted words

Some words aren't for everyone: ceremonial language, sacred stories, things
only certain people may say, or say at certain times. **Leave them out.** A
translator can repeat what it learned, to anyone, at any time — so if words
shouldn't travel, they shouldn't be taught.

When you're not sure, ask the people who would know, and leave them out until
you are. Enthusiastic agreement is the bar, not the absence of an objection.

## Choose how to spell

If people write the language in different ways, the translator learns a
muddle. Before you begin, agree on one way to spell it for the project — the
system a school uses, or a community's standard — and say which dialect the
sentences come from. The tools don't check spelling for you yet, so this is a
human decision, made once and written down.

## Checking drafts

When the rails hand a speaker a sheet of drafts:

- **Y** — the draft is right, the way you'd say it.
- **N** — it's wrong. Then write how you *would* say it. That correction is
  the most useful sentence there is: it's exactly where the translator went
  wrong.
- **?** — not sure. That's fine.

A draft can be right and still sound different from how you'd say it. Mark
what's right as right; write a better way if you have one.

From the second round, a few rows come back from earlier sheets on purpose —
the sheet says so, but not which. Mark them fresh, as if new. Nobody marks
perfectly the same twice, and that's fine: how often the marks agree just
tells everyone how far the results can be trusted.
