# What this is

The big translation apps skip most of the world's languages, or get them
badly wrong. If yours is one of them, you can build your own translator —
from the sentences, word lists and stories your family or community already
has, on your own computer.

An AI assistant can walk you through every step, and you make the decisions
that matter. At every step you'll know how close its translations come to the
way a speaker would say it — and the big results are tested on sentences a
speaker checked.

## What you start with

Whatever you already have:

- a spreadsheet of sentences with their translations,
- a word list or a dictionary,
- a teacher's lesson sheets, or recordings someone has written down,
- and, most valuable of all, someone who speaks the language and can check
  what the translator writes.

Nothing written down yet? A speaker is enough to start. [Before you
begin](/docs/before-you-begin) shows how.

You don't need to convert anything first: the tools read spreadsheets, Word
tables and dictionary files as they are. They also point out rows that look
wrong — a translation that slipped a line, two columns swapped, the same
sentence twice — without changing anything, so someone who knows the data can
decide.

## What happens

You start a project, and the tools take it from there, one step at a time:
bring in your sentences, set some aside to test with, teach your first
translator, try it, and make it better.

Your AI assistant does the fiddly parts. It stops and asks you whenever a
decision is yours: who these words belong to, whether a translator may learn
from them, whether to install something, what counts as good enough. Nothing
like that is ever decided for you.

The assistant needs to be one that works on your computer, like the desktop
app of an AI assistant — a phone app can't run the tools. Most assistants
send what they read to the company that runs them, so the tools only ever
tell yours *how many* sentences are in your private files, never the
sentences themselves — though an assistant that can use your computer can
open files itself. If
some words must stay private, don't ask your assistant to open those files.
[Your words, your rules](/docs/your-words-your-rules) explains.

## What you get

A translator that runs on your own computer. You can try it in your web
browser, use it to draft translations, and — when you decide it's ready, and
the people who hold the words agree — put it into an app or a website.

**Your first translator will probably be a phrasebook:** good with sentences
like the ones it learned from, and shaky with anything new. That's how every
project starts. Then it grows, round by round. A speaker marks its drafts
right or wrong, the tools suggest the kinds of sentences most likely to help
next, and each round tells you whether it really got better.

No community has taken a real language all the way to a working translator on
the rails yet. If you start now, you're among the first.
[What it can't do yet](/docs/what-it-cant-do-yet) tells you what to expect.

## Who it's for

Families, teachers, language programs, community offices, and the people who
help them — anyone with a language the big translators miss. It was built
with low-resource and Indigenous language communities in mind: languages with
few written sentences, and peoples who decide for themselves what happens to
their words.

## What it costs

The tools are free for families, schools, charities, and public services like
hospitals. If a speaker community wants to use them commercially — a language
app, a paid service — ask, and the licence is free.

You need a computer. Any recent one can start. A newer Mac (one with an M1
chip or later) with 16 GB of memory, or a computer whose NVIDIA graphics card
has 16 GB, can build the stronger kind of translator — or a lighter version
of it with 8 GB. (On Windows, the training tools run inside WSL, Windows'
built-in Linux.) Other computers build a much smaller kind. If you use an AI assistant, it may have its own cost.

## What it is not

It isn't an app you type into to get a translation today. It's the workshop
where you build one. And it doesn't promise your translator will be good. It
promises you'll always know where it stands, and what would make it better.

The name comes from Jean-François Champollion, who learned to read Egyptian
hieroglyphs with the help of Coptic — the last form of the Egyptian language,
still kept alive in the prayers of the Coptic Church. A language kept alive
turned out to be the key. [Why that name](/docs/the-name).
