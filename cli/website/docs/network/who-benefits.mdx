# Who Benefits

> **Executive Summary.** Fewer than 600 of the world's roughly 7,000 living languages
> have machine translation of any kind, and more than half of those languages
> are at risk (per Glottolog's Agglomerated Endangerment Status;
> champollion-derived). If Champollion succeeds — a universal,
> community-owned translation layer — the payoff is twofold: communication
> today for speakers the industry skips, and a working tool in the
> preservation and revitalization of at-risk languages. The strategy is
> deliberate: crowdsourced method development measured by open rules,
> sponsored corpus building at published rates, and communities holding the
> keys to their data, their test sets, and any method validated against them.
> This page says what each group gains and what to do first.

## Speakers and their communities \{#speakers}

If you speak an underserved language and English, your judgment is the most
valuable thing in this entire project — more than any model or metric.
Computers can count how similar two sentences look; they cannot tell whether a
sentence is something a real speaker would say. That judgment is yours, and
it's the ground truth everything else here is checked against.

**Speaker validation is paid work at published professional rates ($50–65
CAD/hour), on your schedule, with no technical expertise required** — if you
can read both languages and judge a sentence, you are qualified. The
[Speaker Validation Protocol](/docs/network/specifications/speaker-validation)
publishes three task types:

| Task | What you do | Time | Published pay |
|------|-------------|------|---------------|
| Rate translations | Read machine translations, rate each for "is it real?" and "does it mean the right thing?" | ~8 hours, spread over weeks | $400–520 CAD |
| Review "equivalent" pairs | Say whether two phrasings really mean the same thing | ~2 hours | $100–130 CAD |
| Review rejected words | Check words a grammar tool flagged as invalid — was it right? | ~1.5 hours | $75–100 CAD |

There is also corpus work — creating reference translations of everyday
sentences — paid at the same hourly rates
([details](/docs/network/perspectives/how-speakers-get-paid)). The terms, in
one place: **paid, not volunteer** (payment within two weeks of each task
block, paid regardless of what the results show); **credit on your terms**
(named or anonymous, optional co-authorship); **you can withdraw** your
contributions at any time, with veto power over publication of results you
find problematic.

:::note[Where this stands today]
Honesty first: the pay rates and terms above are published commitments, but
[no community validation round has run yet](/docs/network/honest-limitations)
— the first engagements are being set up. Reaching out now means signing up
to be among the first, not joining an existing program.
:::

**First actions:** do something today by adding sentences to
[Tatoeba](https://tatoeba.org) — Champollion never collects or hosts corpora
itself (test data is always
[fetched from its source](/docs/network/sovereignty/registering-corpora)),
but several benchmarks here are built from Tatoeba sentences and open
collections like [OPUS](https://opus.nlpl.eu/). Then read
[How Speakers Get Paid](/docs/network/perspectives/how-speakers-get-paid)
(five minutes, all the numbers), and reach out —
[info@champollion.dev](mailto:info@champollion.dev) or an issue on the
[public repository](https://github.com/gamedaysuits/Champollion/issues) —
saying what language you speak and what varieties you're comfortable judging.

## Language communities building corpora \{#communities}

The strongest position a community can hold in this system is **owning the
benchmark itself**. A test set is the answer key: whoever holds it decides
what "good translation" means for the language, and every method — ours, a
corporation's, anyone's — is measured against *your* standard.

The ground rule comes first: **your language data is yours.** Registering a
corpus means publishing a descriptive metadata card — never uploading the
corpus — in the [exposure lane you
choose](/docs/network/sovereignty/registering-corpora): open, gated, or fully
sovereign (a test set we never see, scored on your infrastructure). Corpus
building and translation review are professional work
[paid at published rates](/docs/network/perspectives/how-speakers-get-paid),
and payment does not buy your data. When a translation method built for your
language is validated here, [ownership
transfers](/docs/network/sovereignty/ownership-transfer) to your community's
governance organization — code, weights, and deployment control. Even 50
curated translation pairs are enough to open a new leaderboard track.

The full door for communities — sovereignty guarantees, what the Network asks
and gives back, and the step-by-step start — is
**[For Language Communities](/docs/network/community/for-language-communities)**,
with [Run a Sovereign
Contest](/docs/network/sovereignty/run-a-sovereign-contest) as the runbook for
a community-controlled evaluation.

## Translation services & MT vendors \{#vendors}

If you build or sell translation — an MT engine, an LLM pipeline, a
rule-based system, or a human translation service — you can benchmark it here
on published test sets, appear on the public leaderboard and map, and be
judged by the same rules as everyone else. **No fee, no partnership
agreement, no preference for any technology:** the harness is method-neutral
by design. You pay only your own compute or API costs
([how the work is funded](/docs/network/sovereignty/economic-model)).

Most public MT benchmarks cover the same few hundred high-resource languages,
and most are contaminated. Champollion's catalogue registers <SSOTCount field="registryDatasetCount" />
evaluation datasets as sha-pinned, fetch-from-source metadata cards, with
[contamination status disclosed per
dataset](/docs/network/leaderboard/datasets) and a lane for fresh,
never-published test sets. Scores use open, reproducible metrics
(chrF++/BLEU/TER via sacreBLEU; COMET/AfriCOMET reported in a separate neural
lane) under published [scoring
rules](/docs/network/specifications/scoring). Method dependencies are
declared, not hidden, and proprietary engines stay proprietary — the harness
calls your system, never your weights or code. Two honest caveats: **the
board is young** ([Honest Limitations](/docs/network/honest-limitations) says
exactly what is verified today), and **scores do not grant deployment
rights** — for community-stewarded languages, the community decides
deployment ([Data Stewardship](/docs/network/sovereignty/data-sovereignty)).

**First actions (MT engine or pipeline):** pick your pairs from the
[evaluation datasets](/docs/network/leaderboard/datasets) and the
[leaderboard](/leaderboard); `pipx install mt-eval-harness` and follow
[Submit a Method](/docs/network/getting-started/submit-a-method); if your
engine isn't reachable through OpenRouter, implement the
[TranslationMethod protocol](/docs/network/specifications/methods#eval-harness-translationmethod-protocol)
or expose it as a small API service
([guide](/docs/guides/serving-a-method)); then run and publish — community
submissions publish at the self-benchmarked trust tier, clearly labeled,
under the [leaderboard rules](/docs/network/leaderboard/rules).

**If you sell human translation:** where no machine is reliable for a pair,
the site says so plainly and shows consent-gated human providers instead —
read the [human services lane](/human-services), then request a listing by
email ([info@champollion.dev](mailto:info@champollion.dev)) stating the pair
and variety you cover, your terms, and a consent confirmation. Your contact
details are never published without consent. Human translation is also a
method here like any other — some teams benchmark their post-editing
pipelines to show buyers the delta over raw MT.

## Researchers \{#researchers}

The most expensive way to bring machine translation to a low-resource
language is the way the field usually tries: build a training corpus. That
means hundreds of thousands of sentence pairs — far too many for anyone to
check — so quality varies in ways nobody can see, and the failure surfaces
only at the end, when a model trained on those pairs gets wrong exactly what
the data got wrong. Months of collection work can produce a corpus you
cannot fully trust and a model you cannot ship.

An evaluation set inverts those economics. A development and held-out test
set for the same language is on the order of a **thousand** pairs — small
enough that professional curation is actually possible, every pair checked
by someone who knows the language. Seven hundred thousand pairs you can't
verify, or a thousand you can: the eval set is the one deliverable where a
researcher's judgment reaches every sentence. And unlike a training corpus,
it doesn't go stale — every new model, engine, or method that appears can be
measured against it, indefinitely.

This is the bet the whole project is built on — the **generational-pair
thesis**: build the evaluation standard and the incentive, and the community
builds the working solution. You curate the thousand pairs. A prize posted
against your held-out set makes the language a target any method builder can
aim at. The leaderboard measures every attempt in public. One generation of
careful curation opens open-ended competition on everything else.
[Corpus Partnership](/docs/network/specifications/corpus-partnership) is the
working document for exactly this — corpus sizes, workflow, speaker payment —
and the [Prize Specification](/docs/network/specifications/prizes) is the
incentive half.

The measuring tools are ready today. `pipx install mt-eval-harness` gets you the
[eval harness](/docs/network/specifications/harness): deterministic scoring,
fingerprinted [run cards](/docs/network/specifications/run-card), paired
bootstrap [significance](/docs/network/specifications/significance), and a
publish path to the leaderboard. The
[scoring spec](/docs/network/specifications/scoring) documents every metric
with its validation status — externally validated, proxy-validated, or
engineering heuristic — and keeps the composite deterministic; neural
metrics report in a separate lane, never silently folded in. Standard
metrics ride on sacreBLEU and COMET/AfriCOMET, extended with structural
checks (FST validity) that surface what character-overlap metrics miss in
polysynthetic languages. The harness is open-source AGPL-3.0; the CLI is
PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0 — source-available, free for noncommercial use.

Behind the tools sit the registries.
**<SSOTCount field="registryDatasetCount" /> evaluation datasets**, each a
sha-pinned, fetch-from-source metadata card, catalogued in
[Evaluation Datasets](/docs/network/leaderboard/datasets);
**<SSOTCount field="catalogLanguageCount" /> language cards** where
[every field names its source](/docs/reference/language-card-spec) and
disagreeing sources are all shown
([correction procedure](/docs/reference/language-card-citation-procedure));
a license rollup of
**<SSOTCount field="licensedSourceCount" /> registered upstream sources**,
with non-commercial corpora carved out of any commercial, prize, or API lane
by construction. Contamination is a first-class property: public benchmarks
presumed seen by frontier models run in a **relative-only lane**, fresh
community corpora are sealed and segmented with hash manifests
([corpus partnership §4](/docs/network/specifications/corpus-partnership#4-cryptographic-sealing-and-sandbox-testing)),
and communities can hold sovereign benchmarks we never see. The threat model
is stated in the
[corpus design framework](/docs/network/specifications/corpus-design).

Three ways in, by what you want to build. A **corpus**: start from the
eval-first path above — [Corpus Partnership](/docs/network/specifications/corpus-partnership)
end to end, budgeting paid speaker time from day one. A **method**:
implement the
[TranslationMethod protocol](/docs/network/specifications/methods) and follow
[Submit a Method](/docs/network/getting-started/submit-a-method) — about 15
minutes with an OpenRouter key. A **metric**: propose it through the
harness's plugin surface (issue or PR on the
[repository](https://github.com/gamedaysuits/Champollion); the bar for
entering the composite is in the scoring spec). Whichever door, read
[Honest Limitations](/docs/network/honest-limitations) first — including
that community speaker-validation of the metrics has not happened yet — and
the [microeval methodology](/docs/network/context/microeval-methodology) and
[MT evaluation landscape](/docs/network/context/mt-evaluation-landscape)
before designing anything.

**Citing Champollion:** there is no Champollion paper yet — please don't cite
one. Cite the repository and the specific versioned specification or
sha-pinned dataset card you used:

```bibtex
@software{champollion,
  author  = {Forbes, Curtis},
  title   = {Champollion: open evaluation infrastructure for machine
             translation in low-resource languages},
  url     = {https://github.com/gamedaysuits/Champollion},
  year    = {2026},
  note    = {Specifications at https://champollion.dev/docs/network}
}
```

If you use an upstream corpus through our registry, **cite the corpus's own
creators** — the dataset card carries the upstream attribution and license.

## Funders & sponsors \{#funders}

**Champollion is non-commercial.** Sponsorship funds corpus building, tools,
and community work — **100% pass-through, publicly accounted, none of it to
Champollion**: no overhead percentage, no revenue share, no claim on
community assets. Money in becomes paid language work out — it goes to
speakers, corpus builders, and prize winners — and infrastructure costs are
currently carried by the founder
([How the Work Is Funded](/docs/network/sovereignty/economic-model)).

The honest starting point: everything here describes mechanisms that are
**specified and ready, not programs already running at scale.** There are no
grants or sponsors yet, the leaderboard is live and still early, and the prize mechanism
is a [proposed specification](/docs/network/specifications/prizes) — no prize
is open today. What a funder gets from that honesty: published unit costs, a
claims ledger, and specifications you can hold us to.

**Lane 1 — corpus sponsorship.** The scarcest asset in low-resource MT is a
trustworthy test set, and the costs are published:

| What a dollar buys | Published cost | Where it goes |
|--------------------|---------------|----------------|
| A benchmark corpus for one language (50–150 curated entries) | $2,500–6,000 CAD | Bilingual speaker time at $50–65/hr — the largest line item, deliberately |
| A metric-validation round (three speakers checking the automated metrics against human judgment) | $1,475–1,920 CAD | Paid speaker review tasks |
| Method-output review | $500–1,500 CAD | Same published hourly rates |
| Full language onboarding, all-in | $3,350–8,500 CAD | See [Benchmark Specification §10](/docs/network/specifications/benchmark) |

The asset outlives the grant: the corpus belongs to its builders/stewards,
not to Champollion — registered as metadata,
[content never hosted](/docs/network/sovereignty/registering-corpora), under
the steward's license and lane choices. For academic funders, the
[Corpus Partnership Strategy](/docs/network/specifications/corpus-partnership)
is the department-shaped version of this lane.

**Lane 2 — prize sponsorship.** An achievement prize funds a **result, not
an attempt**: money is released only when a method demonstrably clears a
defined, method-neutral bar, with paid community speaker review part of every
claim. Per the [Prize Specification](/docs/network/specifications/prizes),
funds are **sponsor-held** — they stay with the sponsoring organization, or
with a community trust or third-party escrow that the sponsor designates, and
**never with Champollion**, which coordinates measurement and touches no
money. **No prize funds are held anywhere today.** If nobody clears the bar,
nobody is paid; an expired prize stays with the sponsor, to be redirected or
withdrawn at their discretion.
Winning methods transfer to community ownership under the prize's terms.

What a funder can measure: corpora built and registered under steward
control; paid speaker-hours delivered to language communities; language pairs
covered by trustworthy public benchmarks; methods measured and, where prize
terms say so, transferred to community ownership.

**First actions:** read
[How the Work Is Funded](/docs/network/sovereignty/economic-model) (ten
minutes, the full model); pick a lane on
[Get Involved — sponsors](/get-involved#sponsors) — corpus work, a prize, or
compute/API credits for the [public benchmark queue](/contribute); then talk
to us at [info@champollion.dev](mailto:info@champollion.dev) or on the
[public repository](https://github.com/gamedaysuits/Champollion/issues).
We'll answer with specifics, including the parts that aren't built yet.

## Press & media \{#press}

Champollion is a non-commercial research project building trustworthy
machine-translation evaluation for every language — source-available and free
for noncommercial use, with an open-source evaluation harness and registries —
infrastructure for answering one question honestly: *how good is machine
translation for this language, really?* It publishes the test-set registry,
the scoring rules, the harness that runs them, and a public leaderboard and
map showing who can translate what and how well — every method welcome,
human and machine, judged by the same open rules. Where communities hold the
language data, they hold the keys.

What Champollion is **not**: not commercial (no product, no paid API, no
metering, no revenue share, no venture funding —
[self-funded by its founder](/docs/network/sovereignty/economic-model)); not
a data harvester (corpus content is **never hosted**; the registry holds
sha-pinned metadata cards, and community corpora remain the steward's
property outright); not an AI translation product (Champollion measures
translation quality; the companion CLI is a developer tool); and not finished
— the leaderboard is live but young, community programs are being established, and
we would rather be quoted accurately as early-stage than generously as
further along.
