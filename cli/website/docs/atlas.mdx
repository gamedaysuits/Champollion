# The Atlas

**The Atlas is the language index: <SSOTCount field="catalogLanguageCount" />
cards, one per language, where every value names the source it came from.**

It is the reference layer the rest of Champollion stands on. The
[CLI](/docs/intro) reads it to know how a language behaves. The
[Arena](/docs/arena) reads it to know what a benchmark is a benchmark *of*. The
[Network](/docs/network/) is drawn over it. And it is useful entirely on its
own: for most of the languages in it, the card is the most complete cited
summary of the language's situation available in one place.

The cards are generated from their sources, not written by hand. Nobody sits
down and types what they know about a language.

---

## Champollion is an index, not an arbiter

This is the rule the whole Atlas is built to obey, and it is stricter than it
sounds.

Every value on a card cites its source and reports what that source **actually
says** — not what we think is true, not a corrected version, not a rounded
figure. When sources disagree, the card shows **all of them, attributed**.
Speaker counts disagree constantly: Wikidata says one number, Ethnologue
another, a national census a third, a regional survey a fourth. A card carries
each of them with its source and its date. We do not average them, we do not
adjudicate between them, and we do not quietly pick the one that reads best.

The reason is not modesty. A card that manufactured a consensus would be a new
and unciteable claim about somebody's language, invented by us. Preserving the
disagreement keeps the card checkable against the world, and it leaves the
judgement where it belongs — with the reader, and with the community whose
language it is.

Three reading rules follow, and they are worth knowing before you use a card
for anything:

- **`null` means unknown, not inapplicable.** An empty field means we have not
  found a cited source for it yet. It is an invitation, not a verdict. Where a
  field genuinely does not apply, the card says so in words.
- **Derived values say they are derived.** Anything Champollion computes — a
  median, a composite, a recoded flag, a count extracted from a dataset — is
  stamped `champollion-derived` with a note naming what it was derived *from*.
  It is never filed under the upstream dataset's name, because that would put
  our arithmetic in someone else's mouth.
- **Citation beats coverage.** A card with fewer, well-sourced fields is
  healthier than a full one padded from tertiary sources. Wikipedia,
  LLM-generated text and self-knowledge are not primary sources here, and are
  not accepted as ones.

The [Citation Procedure](/docs/reference/language-card-citation-procedure) sets
out which source is authoritative for which kind of claim, and in what order.

## What a card asserts — and what it never asserts

A card makes exactly two kinds of statement.

**1. Properties of the language.** Classification and family, phonological
inventory, typology, script and orthography, vitality and endangerment status,
speaker estimates. Each cited, each labelled derived or asserted.

**2. Existence of a resource.** That a corpus, a model, an FST, a dictionary, a
metric plugin or a translation method *exists* for this language, and at what
access level. This is a capability claim, not a quality claim.

And there is a hard line it does not cross:

:::danger[Measured scores are never on a language card]
Any **measured score of method output** — chrF++, BLEU, COMET, TER, FST
acceptance rate, "percent morphologically valid" — is a **run result**, keyed to
a specific method, dataset and metric. It belongs on the
[leaderboard](/leaderboard), inside [the Arena](/docs/arena), attached to a
fingerprinted run card. It is **forbidden on a language card**, and a linter
rejects any card that carries one.
:::

The distinction is the difference between "an FST exists for this language" — a
fact about the world, true or false independent of us — and "this method scores
0.82 FST acceptance" — a measurement, true only of one run, one dataset version
and one method version. Collapse the two and a card silently becomes a quality
claim about a language, permanently, with no run behind it and no way to
retract it. So they do not collapse. The Atlas tells you **what exists**; the
Arena tells you **how well it works**.

Two more invariants are enforced the same way: a tone claim must not contradict
the card's own phonological inventory, and a displayed speaker count must equal
one of the cited estimates on the card (or be stamped as our own derivation).
The full schema, with every field and its rules, is the [Language Card
Specification](/docs/reference/language-card-spec).

## What is in it, and where it comes from

<SSOTCount field="catalogLanguageCount" /> languages are catalogued, built from
<SSOTCount field="licensedSourceCount" /> registered upstream sources whose
licences are tracked individually — because a card is only redistributable if
everything on it is, and non-commercial sources are walled out of commercial
lanes by construction rather than by good intentions.

The catalogue includes sign languages as the natural languages they are,
includes the constructed languages ISO 639-3 recognizes, excludes programming
languages, and benchmarks individual languages rather than macrolanguage
umbrellas — because translation resources are variety-specific, and a score
attached to an umbrella code would be a claim about varieties nobody evaluated.
Where taxonomies disagree, the card shows the disagreement rather than picking a
winner. Each of those choices is argued, with its consequences, in [What Counts
as a Language Here?](/docs/network/context/what-counts-as-a-language).

**Coverage is not quality, and existence is not coverage.** A card listing three
resources for a language is telling you three things exist — not that any of
them is usable, and not that the language is served. How the project counts
coverage, and what it refuses to count, is [Counting
Coverage](/docs/network/context/coverage-counting).

search the cards directly through the [MCP

## When a card is wrong

Assume some of them are. An index this size, assembled from sources that
disagree with each other, will contain errors — and the ones that matter most
are usually invisible to us and obvious to a speaker.

**Every card carries a "Suggest a correction or addition" link**, which opens
the correction form with the language pre-filled. Bring a source if you have
one; for a community removal or restriction request, no citation is required and
it is handled with priority.

Corrections are applied **at the generator, not to the card**. Cards are
regenerated from their sources, so a hand-edit would be silently overwritten at
the next build — fixing the source is what makes a correction permanent, and it
usually fixes the same defect on every other card that shares that source. This
is slower than editing a file and it is the only version that lasts. The
mechanics are in [Submit to the
Index](/docs/network/getting-started/submit-to-the-index).

**If you are a community member or authority** and want data about your language
restricted, changed or removed, that request does not need to be justified to us
and does not go into a public queue if you would rather it did not. Language data
is biodata: it carries the identity and relationships of the people who speak it,
and it cannot meaningfully be anonymized. We are **sovereignty-aspirant, not sovereignty-certified** — we build the mechanisms and describe them honestly, and
whether they honour community ownership and control of language data is a
determination that belongs to the communities
involved, not to us. The full
position is [Data Stewardship](/docs/network/sovereignty/data-sovereignty), and
the community door is [For Language
Communities](/docs/network/community/for-language-communities).
