# Bring what you have

A translator learns from **pairs**: a sentence in one language, and the same
sentence in the other. This page is about bringing yours in — and about the
questions you'll be asked for each file, which matter more than anything else
on the rails.

## What the tools can read

Bring your files as you keep them. You don't need to convert anything.

| You have | The tools read |
|---|---|
| A spreadsheet of sentences and translations | Excel (`.xlsx`), LibreOffice (`.ods`), `.csv`, `.tsv` or plain text |
| A table in a Word document | The table's rows |
| A dictionary made in Toolbox or FieldWorks (programs many linguists use) | The words — and its example sentences, as pairs |
| Recordings written down in ELAN (a program for transcribing recordings) | The written-down lines and their translations, as pairs |
| A word list | Each word and its translation |
| Notes about the grammar | Kept in their own place, for people and tools to refer to |
| Scripture | Only if you ask, and no translator learns from it until the people who hold it say so |

Your files are read where they are. If a spreadsheet's columns don't say which
language is which, the tools ask you — they never guess from the contents.

The tools also look for rows that seem wrong — a translation that slipped down
a line, two columns swapped, a translation copied from the other side, the
same pair twice, a translation far longer or shorter than usual — and tell
you which rows, by number. They never change or remove anything; someone who
knows the data decides.

## The tools count. They don't read your words out to anyone.

When a file comes in, the tools tell you how many pairs they found and which
columns they used. That's all your AI assistant is shown: counts and column
names, never the sentences themselves.

## The questions for each file

Before anything learns from a file, the rails ask the people who hold it:

- **Who does it belong to?** In your own words — a family, a teacher, a
  community office.
- **Whose words are in it?** The people who said or wrote the sentences. A
  grandmother's notebooks hold her words, even if her grandson typed them.
- **Have they agreed to their use?** *Yes*, *not yet*, or *some said no*.
  The rails keep asking until this is answered. Words from people who haven't
  agreed yet, or who said no, are never used to teach a translator.
- **May a translator learn from it?**
- **May it ever leave this computer?** Files start out private — kept on this
  computer — until you answer.

The answers are written in a small note beside the file, and every tool obeys
it. After your first answer, the tools can only make the rules stricter, never
looser. The one exception: a speaker who hadn't been asked yet can still say
yes.

Your assistant asks these questions; it must never answer them for you. If you
don't know yet — say, you still need to ask an aunt — say *not yet*. The rails
set that file aside and carry on, and ask again later, gently.

## Sentences from other places

If you use sentences you didn't write — from a book, a website, a published
collection — they come with their own rules. Some may be read but not shared;
some may not be used to teach anything. Bring them in the same way, and answer
the questions honestly for them too: the tools will hold them to your answer.
When a collection's licence is unusual or unclear, the tools won't send it to
an outside AI service until its owners have said yes.
