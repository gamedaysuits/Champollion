# Your first translator

This is the exciting part: the first time a translator learns from your
sentences, and you try it. It's also where the rails are most careful, because
the first result is the one people remember.

## Before teaching starts

Your assistant walks through these in order. Most it simply does; it stops
and asks you where a step is yours.

1. **Install the training tools** — once, and only with your yes. They're
   downloaded to your computer.
2. **Gather the sentences into one file** — the tools combine your files,
   keep the strictest of their rules, and leave out exact repeats. Files that
   no translator may learn from are moved aside, not deleted.
3. **Check against the test sentences** — copies and near-copies of your test
   sentences are set aside.
4. **Write down what you expect** — before any result exists, in your own
   words. *"A phrasebook at best"* is a perfectly good prediction. The rails
   won't move on until it's written.
5. **Set aside practice sentences** — a quarter of your sentences while you
   have fewer than 200, then 50, then one in ten once that's more, held back so the translator can be checked as it learns without touching
   the test sentences.
6. **Measure the dictionary** — if you brought a word list, the tools first
   see how far simply looking each word up gets you. Your translator is worth
   using only where it beats that.

## Teaching

Then the translator learns. It runs on your computer and nothing is sent
anywhere. The tools look at your computer's memory and pick what it can
handle: on a newer Mac, or with an NVIDIA graphics card, the stronger kind of
translator (or a lighter version of it, with less memory); on other
computers, a much smaller one. Only one translator learns at a time on a
computer.

It can take a while — minutes for a small one, a few hours for the stronger
kind. It watches itself: if it shows no sign of life at the start, stops
making progress, or runs past a day, it stops and says why. Nothing runs
forever.

## The first result

When it's done, the translator is tested on your test sentences, and you see
a **score**: a number out of 100 for how close its translations come to the
way your speaker said them. Beside it, the tools show a range — how sure they
are — and every warning that applies, word for word.

Then you can try it yourself, in your web browser, with your own sentences.

told it's ready — and the rounds that follow are how you pass it.

## When it's a phrasebook

It almost always is, at first: good with sentences like the ones it learned
from, and shaky with anything new. It can still be useful — for drafts that a
speaker corrects, or for phrases you use again and again. And it's the start
of the rounds that make it better.
