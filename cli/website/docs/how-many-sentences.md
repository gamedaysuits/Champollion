# How many sentences does it take?

It's the first question everyone asks, and the honest answer has two parts:
**more than most people expect** — and **your own rounds will tell you.**

## What we know

We made up a practice language, so a computer could check every answer:
about 2,000 words, perfectly regular grammar, clean data. A translator taught
on a laptop got the exact meaning of new everyday sentences right:

- about **79 in 100** after **6,400** example sentences,
- about **96 in 100** after **25,600**.

Every sentence with a word it had never seen came out wrong — though those
were only about a third of its misses at 6,400 examples. The rest were
grammar it hadn't learned yet.

## What we don't know

How many *your* language needs. A real language has far more everyday words
than our practice one, words with several meanings, and grammar that marks
things English leaves unsaid. So the counts above are a floor: a real language
will need more, and we don't yet know how much more. We're measuring it.

Anyone who gives you a single number for your language, today, is guessing.

## How to estimate yours

Your rounds are the best estimate there is. Once a few rounds — with a better
translator put to use along the way, confirmed by the speaker's blind check —
show a real rise in the speaker's marks, each round report gives a range for
how many more sentences, and how many hours of a speaker's time, it looks
like it would take to reach a translator that gets most new sentences right —
based on how much each doubling of your sentences has helped so far. The
projection updates every round, and it says plainly when the time you have
looks like too little.

## Time

We haven't measured a real speaker's time yet. As a rough guide: if writing
or checking a sentence took about a minute, 6,400 sentences would be about a
hundred hours of a speaker's time — and a real language will need more. That
can sound like a lot. Shared among a family, a class or a community, a couple
of hours a week each adds up — and [other people can help](/docs/join-a-project).

## Make every sentence count

The number of sentences matters less than *which* sentences:

- **Use real sentences.** Things your family actually wants to say beat
  made-up drills. On our practice language, everyday sentences taught more
  than sentences picked to pack in as many new words as possible: after
  3,200 of them, 53 in 100 new sentences came out with the exact meaning
  right, against 39 in 100 — for about the same speaker time.
- **A few sentences for each word.** A word the translator meets only once is
  rarely learned. The rails' wish list names words it has never seen; give
  each one two or three sentences, each saying something different. Fewer
  words, each in a few sentences, teach more than many words once.
- **Aim at the mistakes.** Sentences like the ones it gets wrong teach more
  than random ones. The wish list suggests those too.
- **Keep them short and varied.** Short sentences, many kinds — questions,
  requests, stories.
- **Corrections are gold.** When a speaker marks a draft wrong and writes how
  it should be said, that's exactly where the translator went wrong.
