# Make it better, round by round

Your first translator is a beginning, not an end. From here it grows in
**rounds**. Each round, a speaker checks what it writes, you add the sentences
that would help most, and it learns again. At the end of every round you get
a short report: what changed, how sure the tools are, and what to do next.

This is the heart of the rails. It's also where a speaker's time does the most
good.

## Before the first round: how much time?

The rails start by asking how much time the speaker can give — a week, and in
all. Each round is sized to that. Once a few rounds show a trend, the rails
tell you plainly what that time looks like it can buy, including when it
looks like too little. They never push for more. The speaker's time is theirs.

## One round

1. **A wish list.** The tools look at what the translator gets wrong and make
   a printable sheet of what would help most: words it has never seen — with
   two or three sentences asked for each, since a word met only once is
   rarely learned — and the kinds of sentences it stumbles on. A quarter of the sheet stays open, so you
   still write what *you* need. Use it as much or as little as you like.
2. **Your sentences.** Write 20 or more sentences you'd really want
   translated — things you'd say at home, write in a note, or teach in a
   class. As many as the speaker can check this week.
3. **The translator drafts them.** It writes a translation of each onto a
   sheet for the speaker. While there are few practice sentences, some rows
   have no draft — one in two until there are 50 practice sentences, then one
   in four until there are 200. The speaker writes those, and they become new
   practice sentences (never learned from).
4. **The speaker checks.** For each draft: **Y** if it's right; **N** if it's
   wrong, and then how it should be said; **?** if unsure. Open it in a
   spreadsheet, or print it and type the marks in afterwards.

   From the second round, about one row in ten comes back from an earlier
   sheet, on purpose — the sheet says so at the top, but not which rows.
   Mark them fresh, as if new. How often the marks agree tells everyone how
   far they can be trusted. Those rows cost a few seconds each, and they
   never count toward the round's result.
5. **The tools read the marks.** They count how many drafts were right. The
   fixes, and the drafts marked right, become new sentences to learn from —
   after you've said who they belong to, like any file.
6. **The report.** See below.
7. **Learn again.** With your yes, the translator learns from everything so
   far — usually carrying on from the one you're using, so a round takes less
   time than the first teaching did. The new one is compared with the one
   you're using, sentence by sentence, on the same practice sentences. Only
   if it's better by more than luck do the rails offer it to you — and it
   takes over only when you say yes. Otherwise the one you're using stays,
   and the next round begins. With fewer than 50 practice
   sentences, no score can tell which is better — so the tools don't guess.
   The one you're using stays, and the speaker decides instead (see below).

## The round report

Each report starts with the progress, then the checks, then what to do:

- **The speaker's share of drafts marked right** — the number that matters
  most — with a range showing how sure the tools are.
- **The practice score** — a number out of 100 for how close the translator
  comes, on practice sentences it never learned from — and whether the new
  translator really beat the old one.
- **A check that it isn't just memorizing** — whether the gains on practice
  sentences also show up on new sentences.
- **How far the marks can be trusted** — once ten rows shown again have been
  marked: how often the speaker marked them the same way, and whether drafts
  once marked wrong are now being marked right more often, which can happen
  as anyone grows used to a translator. That's no one's fault, and it's worth
  knowing.
- **How far to "working"** — once the rise is real, a range for how many more
  sentences and hours it looks like, each time your sentences double. The
  range also takes in how far the speaker's marks can be trusted.
- **Two to four things to do next** — like *write sentences with these words*,
  *mark this sheet*, *try the stronger translator*, or *ask for help through
  Join a project*.

Your assistant is told to relay all of it with every number's meaning, to
celebrate only gains the tools measured, and to say *"no measurable change"*
plainly when that's the result. A round with no measurable change still
counts: the translator you're using stays, and you know where you stand.

## The speaker checks without knowing which is which

Two moments get a blind check on your own sentences:

- **After you put a new translator to use**, the next sheet mixes its drafts with
  drafts from the one it replaced — picked at random, not labelled. A rise
  counts as real progress only when the speaker's own marks agree.
- **When the practice sentences can't decide** — there are fewer than 50, or
  the round showed no measurable change — half of the next sheet's drafts
  come from the new translator. If the speaker clearly prefers it, the rails
  offer to put it to use (you still say yes), and the report says it was
  chosen on the speaker's marks.

Either way, the speaker marks every draft the same way, without knowing which
translator wrote it.

## When the rounds stop moving

Two rounds in a row with no rise you can see doesn't mean the rounds stopped
helping. Once a translator is fairly good, a round may add less than your
practice sentences can show. The report says so plainly — it can't tell a
small gain from none — and points at the faster ways forward: a stronger
translator, or more sentences of the kinds it gets wrong.

If you started with the small kind of translator, your computer can teach the
stronger kind, and two rounds in a row since you last changed translators
show no measurable gain, the rails offer to switch. (Rounds with too few
practice sentences to tell don't count.) It's a bigger download and takes
longer to teach, so the rails ask: *yes*, or *not now*. If you say yes, it
learns from everything you have, and it's offered to you only if it's
measurably better — you still say yes before it takes over.

## People who haven't agreed yet

If some of your sentences come from people who haven't agreed yet, the rails
leave those aside and carry on. Each session, they ask once, gently, whether
everyone has agreed. When the answer is yes, those sentences join the next
round.
